How to choose a niche for your videos

Last updated 13 October 2026.

A niche is a short answer to two questions: who are your videos for, and what do they get from watching? With an answer, every new video is easier to plan, easier for the right people to find, and easier to keep making. Without one, you start from scratch every time.

A good niche sits where three things overlap: something you know, something people want explained, and something you could keep talking about for a long time.

Start with what people already ask you

The best place to start isn’t a list of trending topics. It’s the questions people already bring to you. If friends, customers, students or colleagues keep asking you the same things, that shows the topic is wanted and that you can explain it.

Spend ten minutes writing down:

  • Questions people asked you in the last month, word for word if you can remember them.
  • Things you explain at work so often you could do it in your sleep.
  • Something you learned the hard way that you wish someone had explained to you sooner.

Look for a pattern. The topic that keeps coming up is usually your strongest starting point, even if it feels too ordinary to you. What is obvious to you is often exactly what someone else is searching for.

Pick a viewer, not just a topic

A topic tells people what you talk about. A viewer tells them whether it is for them. “Cooking” is a topic, and there are millions of cooking videos. “Quick, cheap meals for students in their first year away from home” is a niche, and the right person knows it is for them at once.

TopicNiche
MoneyMoney basics for people who just got their first salary
HealthSimple health habits for busy parents
TechnologyHow everyday tech works, explained for people who don’t like tech
FarmingSmall-plot farming tips for first-time growers
BusinessRunning a market stall: pricing, stock and selling online

Narrow feels risky, but it is how small channels grow. You can widen later, once people know you for one thing.

Check you could make 30 videos on it

Set a timer for twenty minutes and write down 30 video titles in your niche. Write each one as a question someone would type into search.

  • Stuck at ten: the niche is too narrow. Widen the viewer or the topic a little.
  • Easy to reach 30, and they could be about anything: it is too broad. Narrow the viewer.
  • Reached 30, and they clearly belong together: you have a niche. Keep the list, because it is your first few months of videos.

See who else is making it

Search for a few of your titles on YouTube and TikTok. Other people making videos on your topic is a good sign: it means there is an audience. What you are looking for is a gap.

  • Are the existing videos too long, too technical, or made for a different country or audience?
  • Read the comments. Questions people ask there that nobody has answered are ready-made video ideas.
  • Notice what you would do differently. That difference is your angle.

If you can’t find anyone making videos on it, check that people are searching for it at all before you commit.

Make sure you’ll still care in a year

Channels grow slowly, and most people give up before it pays off. Choose something you would happily talk about every week for a year even if the first videos get few views. Interest is easy to hear in a voice, and so is boredom.

Test it with five short videos

You don’t have to get the niche right on paper. Make five short videos from your list of 30, post them over two or three weeks, and see what happens.

  • Look at how much of each video people watch, not just how many people watch it. A video people finish is a topic people want.
  • Notice which videos get shared, saved or commented on.
  • Make more of what worked. Drop or rework what didn’t.

Five videos are enough to learn from, but not enough to judge the whole niche. Give a niche you believe in at least a few months before deciding it isn’t working.

Write your one-line promise

Finish by putting your niche into one sentence. It becomes your channel description, your bio and the test for every new idea: if a video doesn’t keep the promise, it belongs somewhere else.

I help [who] to [do or understand what] with [what kind of videos].

For example:

  • I help first-time salary earners make sense of their money with two-minute explainers.
  • I help busy parents build healthier habits with short, practical videos.
  • I help market traders sell more with simple tips they can use the same day.
